# PAP, RADs, and Home Mechanical Ventilators
## How an epidemic, a blower, and a sentence in a 1993 tax bill produced three device categories

**Audience:** pulmonary & critical care fellows · **Format:** 45 min (40 content + 5 discussion) · **Target:** 22 slides
**Policy verified:** September 18, 2026

---

## READ THIS BEFORE BUILDING THE DECK — one premise needs correcting

The story you asked for is *directionally right and better documented than the usual telling*, but the mechanism is different, and the difference is the most interesting thing in the talk.

**The common telling:** Medicare built home-ventilator coverage for polio survivors, then feared the sleep-apnea population would overwhelm it, so it invented a separate RAD category.

**What the primary sources actually support:**

1. **The polio premise is sound but rests on expert recollection, not archive.** Gusman & Wolfe state plainly that the home-ventilation NCDs were written in the 1960s–70s, deliberately left vague, "because the assumption was that these criteria would only be relevant to patients with chronic respiratory failure secondary to poliomyelitis… it was thought that these criteria would not be in use for long." That paragraph carries **no citation** in their paper. Present it as *"as Wolfe and colleagues recount it."* The structural consequence — a vague, generous, uncapped polio-era ventilator benefit sitting next to detailed 1998 RAD criteria — is independently verifiable from the two coverage frameworks themselves.

2. **The separation was statutory and coding-driven, not clinical and not sleep-apnea-driven.** §13543 of the **Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993** amended §1834(a)(3)(A) to exclude "intermittent assist devices with continuous positive airway pressure devices" — by name — from the frequent-and-substantial-servicing (FSS) category. Every bilevel device was swept out of the ventilator payment category by an act of Congress. The Trust Fund logic is explicit in the 2006 rule: FSS paid **$38,530 over five years** for one device for one patient versus **$12,201** under capped rental — "This difference in costs highlights the fact that the correct classification of these devices for Medicare payment purposes is a significant issue in terms of **safeguarding the Medicare Trust Fund**." So: yes, fiscal protection. No, not framed as sleep apnea.

3. **CMS explicitly refused to engage the volume argument.** A commenter proposed that ventilator spending growth reflected the newly recognized benefits of NPPV rather than miscoding. CMS: *"The reasons for the growth in expenditures for respiratory assist devices are **not relevant** to this final rule. The law requires that these devices be excluded from the DME FSS payment category under Medicare."* Put that quote on a slide.

4. **Where the volume fear *is* documented is one level down — at the MAC medical directors, in 1998.** ONMAP: *"These medical directors would become alarmed if outlays suddenly grew exponentially… **In response to the meteoric rise in BPAP prescriptions and subsequent charges**, the DME MAC medical directors implemented a regional medical review policy based on recommendations of a consensus conference… organized in 1998."* That is your rationing slide, and it is sourceable.

5. **The loop closes, and this is the punchline of the whole talk.** Restrictive RAD criteria written in 1998 out of alarm at bilevel volume made RADs *harder to qualify for than ventilators*. A decade later clinicians and suppliers migrated hypercapnic COPD patients **out of** the capped RAD category and **into** the uncapped ventilator category — producing an **85-fold rise in claims (2,528 → 215,379) and an 89-fold rise in spending ($3.8M → $340M) between 2009 and 2015**. CMS's answer, in 2025, was NCD 240.9. *Rationing the cheap device created the expensive one.*

**Also correct before building:** "Severe COPD" was **removed from LCD L33800 effective June 9, 2025**. Any slide built on the old Group I/Group II severe-COPD LCD criteria is wrong. COPD now runs through NCD 240.9 only.

# SECTION I · The clinical problem, not the equipment catalog
### Slides 1–2 · 0–3 min

### Slide 1 — "Three patients need positive pressure. They do not need the same thing."

Three cards, clinical problem only, **no device names yet**:

| | Consequence of a night without the device |
|---|---|
| **A.** 54M, symptomatic OSA, AHI 38, normal awake ABG, normal strength | Sleepy, hypertensive. Nothing acute. |
| **B.** 68F, COPD, PaCO₂ 58 two weeks after an exacerbation, on 2 L | Progressive CO₂ rise; readmission risk. |
| **C.** 61M, ALS, FVC 42%, now needs support during meals and conversation | Unrecognized interruption may be fatal. |

**Ask:** *What must the system do for each? What happens if it stops?* Do not answer. Return in slide 22.

> *Optional substitution (Brainstorm 2's preference):* open instead with the single multifactorial patient from your OSA-in-hypercapnic-RF review — obesity, smoking, mild emphysema, cardiac abnormalities, never had spirometry or a sleep study. Stronger if you want the talk to land on diagnostic uncertainty; weaker if you want it to land on device categories. **Recommendation: use the three cards** — the talk's spine is categorical, and three cards map onto the three categories you will end with.

### Slide 2 — Four decisions that get collapsed into one

```
Diagnosis  →  Treatment mode  →  Platform capabilities  →  Coverage pathway
```

The correction that must come early: **PAP, RAD, and HMV are not mutually exclusive categories.** Medicare's OSA policy (L33718) defines "PAP device" to mean *both* E0601 CPAP *and* E0470 bilevel-without-backup. The same E0470 code follows an entirely different coverage pathway when the diagnosis is not OSA.

**Learning objectives:**
1. Explain why two engineering lineages converged in capability but not in category.
2. Distinguish mode from platform from billing code.
3. Select and justify a home support system from clinical need and consequence-of-interruption, not from device prestige.

# SECTION II · Lineage one — polio and the invention of sustained ventilation
### Slides 3–6 · 3–13 min

### Slide 3 — Before you can treat hypoventilation, you must be able to see it

Compressed timeline, not a comprehensive history:

- **1832** — first negative-pressure "box" (Dalziel, Scotland)
- **1907** — Dräger's portable positive-pressure/negative-pressure device, mass-produced for **mine rescue and gas poisoning** — positive pressure was in industrial use before it was in clinical use
- **1928–29** — Drinker & Shaw's iron lung *(J Clin Invest 1929;7:229–247)*; prolonged support reported in *Lancet* 1931
- **J.H. Emerson (Cambridge, MA)** brings it to scale
- **1950s** — the diagnostic prerequisite arrives: electrode measurement of blood pH lets Ibsen attribute polio mortality to **quantifiable hypercapnia and acidosis**, not to "bulbar failure"

**Teaching beat:** the problem became treatable when it became *measurable*. Hold that thought for slide 18, where a PaCO₂ number becomes a coverage threshold.

**Visual:** one iron-lung photograph. Ward of tanks, heads protruding, lids lifted for nursing care.

### Slide 4 — 1949 and 1952: two inflection points, not one

- **1948–49, Los Angeles** — Albert Bower (physician) and Ray Bennett (engineer) devise a valve converting tank respirators to deliver **intermittent positive pressure during inspiration**. Clinical application from 1949 improved survival **21% → 84%**. *(D'Cruz & Hart 2024, citing Trubuhovich, Crit Care Resusc 2007)* — **this predates Copenhagen and is almost always omitted.**
- **Copenhagen, 1952** — insufficient iron lungs, and the tanks could not ventilate bulbar polio. **Death rate ~80%**, falling markedly with tracheotomy and manual positive-pressure ventilation — medical students bagging patients in shifts. *(Hind, Polkey & Simonds 2017; Ibsen 1954; Lassen, Lancet 1953)*

**The narrative question that drives the rest of the talk — do not skip it:**
> *"Once a patient survives respiratory paralysis, who supplies the next breath — tomorrow, next month, and at home?"*

**Visual:** one photograph, then a simple four-box diagram: ventilation · monitoring · trained personnel · ongoing support. The machine is one box of four.

### Slide 5 — Survival created a care problem that philanthropy solved first

- **1946** — the March of Dimes (then the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis) **funds and transports thousands of iron lungs**
- **Late 1950s** — MOD is operating **15 polio respiratory centers**
- *"Up until this time, patients with chronic respiratory failure requiring ventilator support were thought to be **destined for lifelong hospitalization**."*
- **Rancho Los Amigos Respiratory Center, Downey, CA** — the first system of ventilatory home care; discharged polio survivors home with iron lungs and portable chest cuirasses. These cuirass and jacket users were **among the earliest domiciliary NIV patients**
- **Salk's vaccine ends the epidemics. By the mid-1960s the MOD closes its respiratory centers for lack of funding.**

⚠️ *Attribution:* Gusman & Wolfe source the MOD and Rancho Los Amigos claims to marchofdimes.org and an independentliving.org history page — usable, but attribute to their secondary account.

**Teaching point:** home ventilation required three things, and only one of them was a machine — **equipment, a care system, and financing.**

### Slide 6 — The handoff: from philanthropy to Medicare, with an expiration date that never came

- **1965** — Medicare established (Social Security Act). *"When it was first established, Medicare did not include coverage for individuals with chronic respiratory failure because at the time, **the polio community was told that the MOD would continue to take care of their respiratory needs**."* When the MOD pivoted to birth defects, the responsibility transferred to Medicare.
- **1960s–70s** — Medicare writes NCDs for home mechanical ventilation. They include **in-home respiratory therapists, consumable supplies, and backup equipment.** No LCDs are written.

**The central quote of the talk — put it on the slide verbatim, with the caveat:**
> *"The NCDs for HMV were **purposely left vague** because the assumption was that these criteria would only be relevant to patients with chronic respiratory failure secondary to poliomyelitis. Since the vaccine was effectively eradicating polio, **it was thought that these criteria would not be in use for long**."*
> — Gusman & Wolfe, *Sleep Med Clin* 2025 · **uncited in the original; expert recollection, not archival record**

- **1977** — the **LP3 portable volume ventilator** is FDA-cleared for use outside the hospital (510(k) K770132). First target population: **ventilator-dependent children**, led by Allen Goldberg, who imported the approach from France to Children's Hospital of Philadelphia *(King 2012, from personal communication — flag it)*.
- **1981** — **Katie Beckett**, an infant left ventilator-dependent by viral encephalitis in an Iowa PICU. Medicaid claimed it could only pay for ICU care. **TEFRA 1982 §134** created the state-plan option (SSA §1902(e)(3)) letting states disregard parental income for children who would otherwise require institutional-level care. Distinguish the **1981 individual intervention** from the **1982 statutory option** — and note this is Medicaid, about *access to home care*, not the later RAD billing story. Keep to 45 seconds.

**Slide takeaway:** the ventilator benefit was designed generously, for a population everyone expected to disappear.

# SECTION III · Lineage two — the blower that treated a different problem
### Slides 7–9 · 13–19 min

### Slide 7 — 1981: nasal CPAP is a splint, not a ventilator

Sullivan, Issa, Berthon-Jones & Eves, *Lancet* 1981;1:862–865. The conceptual advance is **pneumatic splinting of the upper airway through a nasal interface** — not replacement of ventilatory work.

**Visual:** upper-airway schematic + a single flat pressure trace. Contrast directly with slide 4's tank.

| | Ventilator lineage | Sleep-apnea device lineage |
|---|---|---|
| Original problem | Respiratory paralysis; the pump fails | Intermittent upper-airway obstruction |
| Engineering answer | Negative pressure → positive pressure → portable home ventilators | A blower, a mask, one pressure |
| Central concern | Reliability; continuity of support | Effective airway treatment through a tolerable interface |
| Failure mode | Death | Sleepiness |

### Slide 8 — 1990: bilevel is the bridge — and it is a descendant of the blower

Sanders & Kern, *Chest* 1990;98:317–324 — independently adjustable inspiratory and expiratory pressures via nasal mask. Separating the two pressures created a route from *treating obstruction* toward *assisting ventilation*.

**The lineage sentence — quote it:**
> *"Cross-pollination from the development of continuous CPAP blowers and masks to treat obstructive sleep apnea led to the increasing use of smaller bilevel pressure support devices. This meant that by the early 1990s, NIV support, whether in the hospital or home, was delivered largely via positive pressure devices."* — Hind, Polkey & Simonds, *AJRCCM* 2017

**And the version that explains everything that follows:**
> *"RADs… **evolved from CPAP machines** for treating OSA. Home mechanical ventilators **evolved from hospital ventilators** and are designed to function as life-support devices."* — Gusman & Wolfe 2025

**Main historical figure — build this first.** Two horizontal tracks converging but never merging. **Do not draw a single ladder from CPAP to RAD to HMV.**

### Slide 9 — The population explodes

- BPAP arrives in the 1990s; use for **severe COPD and neuromuscular disease becomes widespread during that decade** (ONMAP).
- These smaller devices *"opened up the possibility of using home-based ventilatory support for other medical conditions"* — OSA, COPD, CSA, hypoventilation (Gusman & Wolfe).
- **The category cannot see the difference.** King, on 2010 Medicare claims: *"There is no way to distinguish the number of E0470 patients who need ventilator support from those obstructive sleep apnea patients who were non-adherent to CPAP and were therefore moved to a bi-level device for comfort."*

**One number slide.** Medicare claims, 2010 (King 2012, Table 9):

| E0470 bilevel, no backup | E0471 bilevel + backup | All true home ventilators | E0460 negative pressure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **36,117** | **7,793** | **4,071** | **22** |

Twenty-two chest cuirasses still on the fee schedule in 2010. The iron lung never quite left the statute.

# SECTION IV · The fork — why a separate designation exists
### Slides 10–13 · 19–28 min

### Slide 10 — 1992–1993: a manufacturer's sentence, then an act of Congress

- **Jan 1 1992** — HCPCS **E0452**, *"intermittent assist device with continuous positive airway pressure device (CPAP),"* created to describe bilevel devices. The manufacturer's own submission is the hinge of the whole story:
  > *"The word 'intermittent' refers to devices that are designed to be used by the patient for only part of the day, **usually during the hours of sleep**."*
  > *"**The bi-level equipment requires very little maintenance and servicing.**"*
- **Same day** — **E0453**, *"therapeutic ventilator; suitable for use 12 hours or less per day."*
- **1993** — **OBRA §13543** amends SSA §1834(a)(3)(A), excluding "intermittent assist devices with continuous positive airway pressure devices" from the FSS category **by name**. FSS was *"intended to include items which require frequent servicing in order to avoid imminent danger to a beneficiary's health"* (H.R. Conf. Rep. 103-213).

**The money, and the stated reason:**
> *"Total Medicare payments for one device furnished to one patient under the FSS payment category would be as much as **$38,530 after 5 years** as opposed to **$12,201** if the device were classified under the CR payment category. This difference in costs highlights the fact that the correct classification of these devices… is a significant issue in terms of **safeguarding the Medicare Trust Fund**."* — 71 FR 4520

**So: the split was fiscal. It was not clinical, and it was not about sleep apnea by name.**

### Slide 11 — 1998: where the volume fear actually shows up

Two things happen in the same window, and fellows should not conflate them:

**(a) Coverage criteria.** ONMAP, verbatim:
> *"These medical directors would become **alarmed if outlays suddenly grew exponentially** even when new technologies arrived in the marketplace. **In response to the meteoric rise in BPAP prescriptions and subsequent charges**, the DME MAC medical directors implemented a regional medical review policy based on recommendations of a consensus conference of experts organized in 1998 by the ACCP and NAMDRC."*
> *(Conference 1998 → published Chest 1999;116:521–534. Policies implemented **October 1, 1999**.)*

That 1999 policy renamed the category **"respiratory assist device, bi-level pressure capability"** — coining the term fellows still trip over — and split backup-rate from non-backup-rate into separate codes for the first time. **This is the RAD designation's actual birthday.**

**(b) Payment category.** Deliberately deferred. CMS *"delayed our decision regarding the appropriate DME payment category for devices with the backup rate."* The delay ran another 6½ years.

**Code chronology for the appendix:** E0452/E0453 (1992) → K0532/K0533/K0534 (Oct 1 1999) → **E0470/E0471/E0472 (Jan 1 2004)**.

### Slide 12 — 2006: CMS is asked the clinical question and declines to answer it

The public comments made exactly the argument this audience would make. The responses are the best teaching material in the entire regulatory record.

> **Comment:** *"The FDA classifies NPPVs as ventilators and, as such, their purpose and function require monitoring and servicing to avoid risk to the patient's health… Medicare payment policy is the only area where these ventilators are referred to as 'respiratory assist devices.'"*
> **Response:** *"CPAP devices and intermittent assist devices with CPAP are indeed **referred to as ventilators in the statute**, but are nonetheless excluded from the FSS category… **Therefore, the law requires this change.**"* / *"FDA classification… does not determine Medicare coverage and payment rules."*

> **Comment:** growth in ventilator expenditures reflects the benefits of NPPV becoming known after 1995, not miscoding.
> **Response:** ***"The reasons for the growth in expenditures for respiratory assist devices are not relevant to this final rule."***

Backed by **OIG OEI-07-99-00440 (June 11, 2001)**: supplier services *"consist primarily of routine maintenance and patient monitoring"*; actual visits fall short of suppliers' own protocols and *decline over time*; capped rental would have saved **$11.5 million annually**.

**Effective April 1, 2006.** Backup-rate RADs move to capped rental. Fee schedule at the time: **$642.17/month with backup rate vs $256.60 without**; ~$70 million in 2004 device expenditures.

**The resulting two-structure world — the engine of everything after:**

| | RAD (E0470/E0471/E0472) | Home ventilator (E0465–E0468) |
|---|---|---|
| Payment category | **Capped rental** | **Frequent & substantial servicing** |
| Duration | 13 months to purchase option (or 15 + semiannual servicing) | **Monthly rental indefinitely while medically necessary; no cap, no title transfer** |
| Bundling | Device; supplies separately eligible | **All-inclusive** — equipment, accessories, supplies, maintenance, repairs, replacement. Separate billing denied as unbundling |
| Practical consequence | *"Patients on RADs typically do not receive home follow-up visits from a respiratory therapist"* | *"Visited frequently for the first few months post-discharge, then at least every 30–90 days"* (King 2012) |

⚠️ **Nuance ONMAP insists on:** FSS is an **equipment**-servicing payment category, not a guaranteed schedule of professional RT visits. CMS *"will correctly argue that the statute and regulations apply to frequent and substantial servicing for equipment, not professional services."* The RT support is a culture that grew up around the payment, not a benefit the payment defines.

### Slide 13 — The loop closes: rationing the cheap device created the expensive one

**Step 1.** 1998 RAD criteria written restrictively, out of alarm at bilevel volume.
**Step 2.** Those criteria become *harder to satisfy than the ventilator criteria.* Gusman & Wolfe: *"Since the RAD criteria are **paradoxically more restrictive and challenging to fulfill than those for HMV**, this has led to a trend toward providers inappropriately prescribing HMV… particularly for patients with COPD."* ONMAP: *"The clinicians honestly stated that **it was easier to prescribe HMV than a device with S/T mode capability**"* — and they believed the lifelong clinical/technical support that came with HMV was essential.
**Step 3.** Post-2010, post-discharge hypercapnic COPD patients migrate into the uncapped category, coinciding with COPD readmission penalties.

**OIG OEI-12-15-00370, September 2016 — the chart:**

| E0464/E0466 | 2009 | 2015 | Growth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claims | 2,528 | 215,379 | **85×** |
| Spending | $3.8 M | $340 M | **89×** |
| Beneficiaries | 415 | 32,848 | **79×** |

Ventilator ≈ **$1,327–$1,561/month, uncapped**; RAD ≈ **$209–$614/month, capped at 13–15 months**.

> *"The emergence of this multimodal device, when combined with Medicare coverage and payment policies that favor reimbursement for ventilators, may create incentives for suppliers to provide and bill for a ventilator **when the device is actually being used as a RAD or CPAP device**."*

**Balance it, or the talk becomes a fraud story.** Three suppliers accounted for 54% of the 2012–2015 national increase, one of them billing HMV for devices configured as BPAP. But growth alone does not make every additional prescription inappropriate — ONMAP calls the growth *"not unexpected."* **Two competing risks, side by side: under-access to appropriate NIV vs. use of unnecessarily costly equipment.** That tension, not fraud, is the real subject.

**Visual:** the loop as a closed circle — restrictive RAD criteria → HMV substitution → spending growth → CMS response → *(2025)* new criteria.

# SECTION V · Convergence without merger — mode, platform, code
### Slides 14–16 · 28–33 min

### Slide 14 — EPAP, pressure support, and backup breaths solve three different problems

One progressively annotated pressure–time schematic: **CPAP → bilevel S → bilevel S/T**.

- Expiratory pressure → **airway patency**
- IPAP–EPAP difference → **inspiratory assistance / unloading**
- Timed breaths → **insufficient spontaneous triggering**

Small vocabulary box only: **VAPS** (AVAPS, iVAPS) and **ASV** are *control strategies*, not synonyms for HMV, and not synonyms for each other. Rise time, cycling, and manufacturer algorithms are a separate lecture.

### Slide 15 — Similar waveforms do not imply equivalent systems

| Device description | Principal distinction | Code |
|---|---|---|
| CPAP / APAP | Single-level positive pressure | **E0601** |
| Bilevel RAD, no backup rate | Separate IPAP/EPAP, no timed breaths | **E0470** |
| Bilevel RAD with backup rate | Timed support; further algorithms model-dependent | **E0471** |
| Home ventilator, noninvasive interface | Ventilator-class system via mask/mouthpiece | **E0466** |
| Home ventilator, invasive interface | Ventilator-class system via tracheostomy | **E0465** |
| *(Multi-function / dual-function ventilators)* | *+ O₂ concentration, nebulization, suction, cough assist* | *E0467 / E0468* |

**The FDA taxonomy is orthogonal to the CMS taxonomy — and worth 30 seconds.** FDA sorts by intended use and **exhaust type**: exhalation valve vs passive port. `NOU` (continuous ventilator, home use, exhalation valve) devices *"must be locatable in the event of a recall."* `BZD` is *"indicated only for the treatment of adult obstructive sleep apnea."* `MNS` devices assume patients *"should be expected to have no more than minor and transient adverse effects if ventilation/CPAP cannot be provided during extensive periods of time (eg, overnight)."* **That sentence is the real category boundary** — and the FDA drew it around consequence-of-interruption, which is what you actually care about.

### Slide 16 — The convergence, stated three ways

**(a) The blurring was noticed in 2012.** King: *"The distinction between ventilator and RAD is becoming blurred. Newer ICU ventilator designs have effective leak compensation… Conversely, some newer RADs are designed to provide substantial support through a tracheostomy. **Confusion develops when reimbursement schemes are tied to these device features rather than clinical needs and goals.**"*

**(b) The mode you need for guideline-concordant COPD ventilation exists on both sides of the line.** Applying Chatburn's taxonomy, **Bi-Level S/T = PC-IMV(2)s,s** — and *"modes with this TAG are available **both on RADs with backup rate and home mechanical ventilators**, and hence capable of providing high-intensity ventilation for the treatment of COPD with chronic ventilatory failure."* Two devastating corollaries from the same table: **CPAP and "Spontaneous" mode carry an identical TAG (PC-CSVs)**, and **ASV and iVAPS carry an identical TAG (PC-IMV(2)a,a)** despite being marketed as unrelated therapies.

**(c) The proposed fix.** Hatipoğlu, Lewarski & Chatburn: *"The current terminology of noninvasive ventilator modes is based on **manufacturer whims, like the brand names of drugs**… We propose that a paradigm shift… is necessary. Instead of a **device-based** approach, we should adopt a **therapeutic approach based on clinically effective ventilator modes** for that disorder."*

**But convergence is not equivalence.** Four differences remain real and clinically consequential:

| Capability | RAD | HMV |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal battery** | No | **Yes** (internal + external) |
| **Alarms** | Limited | **Sophisticated** |
| **Mouthpiece ventilation** | **No** | Yes |
| **Invasive interface** | No (E0472 excepted) | Yes |
| Pressure ceiling | ~25–30 cm H₂O, model-dependent | Higher (40+; King notes some deliver up to 60 cm H₂O PS, rarely used >30) |
| Modes | S, S/T, some PC and VAPS | Multimodal; **VAPS with auto-EPAP** |

⚠️ **Sources disagree on the RAD ceiling** — 25 (Gusman & Wolfe) vs 30 ("the most advanced RADs," Jimenez & Choi). Say "25–30, model-dependent." The agreed line is **VAPS on RADs; VAPS *with auto-EPAP* only on HMVs.**

**The right question, on the slide:** *"What can this patient **not** safely or effectively do with the simpler system?"*

**The sobering counterweight** — alarms are a capability, not a guarantee. King's 2010 MAUDE review: ≥11 home-ventilation deaths reported; **in at least 5, the ventilator did not alarm.** One narrative: *"Husband unsure if alarm going off or not, 'as there were always alarms going off.'"*

# SECTION VI · Current categories — indications and choosing
### Slides 17–21 · 33–42 min

### Slide 17 — Coverage follows a diagnosis-specific pathway, not an escalation ladder

**Date-stamp this slide: Medicare policy checked September 18, 2026.**

| Clinical indication | Policy to open |
|---|---|
| OSA, CPAP or qualifying bilevel | **LCD L33718** (rev. 01/01/2024) + Article A52467 |
| Neuromuscular / severe thoracic cage disease | **LCD L33800** (rev. 06/09/2025) |
| Central or complex sleep apnea | **LCD L33800** |
| Hypoventilation syndrome (incl. OHS) | **LCD L33800** |
| **Chronic respiratory failure from COPD — RAD *or* HMV** | **NCD 240.9** (eff. **06/09/2025**) |
| Ventilator, neuromuscular / thoracic restrictive | **NCD 280.1** + ventilator coding guidance |

**Three things fellows get wrong:**
1. **NCD 240.9 is narrower than its reputation.** It governs **COPD only** — and governs *both* RAD and HMV for COPD in one document. NMD, chest wall disease, OHS, CSA and OSA all remain under the LCDs. The 2025 NCD did not unify home NIV policy; it **carved COPD out of it**.
2. **"Severe COPD" was deleted from L33800 on 06/09/2025.** The Group I/II construct is gone.
3. **The PaCO₂ threshold is not one number.** **≥52 mmHg** under NCD 240.9 for COPD (RAD *and* HMV); **≥45 mmHg** under L33800 for restrictive thoracic disease and hypoventilation syndrome.

### Slide 18 — What NCD 240.9 actually requires (COPD)

**RAD with backup rate, initial 6 months — all of:**
1. **PaCO₂ ≥52 mmHg** by ABG, awake, on prescribed FiO₂
2. **Sleep apnea is not the predominant cause** — *"Formal sleep testing is not required if, per the treating clinician, the patient does not experience sleep apnea as the predominant cause."* **"Not predominant" ≠ "absent."**
3. Either **stability** (no >1 new/increased respiratory symptom for ≥2 days and no pharmacologic change in the 2 weeks before initiation) **or persistence** (hypercapnia ≥2 weeks post-hospitalization after an exacerbation requiring acute NIV)

By 6 months it must be used as **high-intensity therapy: IPAP ≥15 cm H₂O and backup rate ≥14/min.** *(CMS clarified in April 2026 that "high intensity" applies to RADs only, never to HMV.)*

**The most important door in the NCD — RAD at discharge:** covered immediately for 6 months if the patient required a RAD or ventilator in the **24 hours before discharge** and the clinician judges risk of rapid symptom exacerbation or PaCO₂ rise. **No ABG threshold on this pathway.**

**Continuing coverage (RAD):** by 6 months, **≥4 h/24 h on ≥70% of days in a 30-day period** *and* at least one outcome — PaCO₂ <46, stabilization of a rising PaCO₂, **20% reduction from baseline**, or improvement in headache / fatigue / dyspnea / confusion / sleep quality. Months 7–12: adherence in **each** paid rental month.

**HMV for COPD — must be used in a volume-targeted mode**, plus PaCO₂ ≥52, sleep apnea not predominant, **and at least one of:**
- FiO₂ **≥36% or ≥4 L** nasally
- Support **>8 h per 24 h**
- **Alarms and internal battery** needed because the patient *"is unable to effectively breathe on their own for more than a few hours and the unrecognized interruption of ventilatory support is likely to cause a life-threatening condition"*
- Clinician judgment that none of the four RAD outcomes is achievable *"because the patient's needs exceed the capabilities of a RAD"*

**Two asymmetries worth a sentence each:** HMV continuing coverage requires **adherence only — no clinical outcome**. And for volume-targeted HMV used >8 h/24 h with an oronasal mask at night, **a second daytime interface (mouthpiece or nasal mask) is covered.**

### Slide 19 — The non-COPD pathways, and the one that matters most for fellows

**Restrictive thoracic / neuromuscular (L33800) — the only pathway where the clinician freely chooses E0470 vs E0471, and the only one that does not require hypercapnia.** Documented NMD or severe thoracic cage abnormality, COPD not a significant contributor, **plus any ONE of:**
- awake **PaCO₂ ≥45 mmHg**, **or**
- **SpO₂ ≤88% for ≥5 min** of nocturnal recording (≥2 h recording), **or**
- **NMD only: MIP <60 cm H₂O, or FVC <50% predicted**

> **Say this out loud:** an ALS patient qualifies on **MIP or FVC alone** — normal ABG, no sleep study. Fellows routinely wait for hypercapnia they do not need.

**Hypoventilation syndrome:** PaCO₂ ≥45 awake, **FEV₁/FVC ≥70%**, plus either a **≥7 mmHg** sleep/on-waking rise or SpO₂ ≤88% for ≥5 min **not caused by obstructive events**. E0471 here is a **step-up only** — it requires a covered E0470 already in use and failure on it.

**Central / complex sleep apnea:** requires **attended facility PSG before initiation** (home testing does not qualify) showing CAHI ≥5/h, centrals >50% of events, and **no evidence of hypoventilation** — plus demonstrated improvement on the settings to be prescribed.

**OSA (L33718):** E0470 requires E0601 **tried and proven ineffective despite optimal mask fit and pressure**. **E0471 is categorically denied if the primary diagnosis is OSA.** Adherence: **≥4 h/night on 70% of nights in a consecutive 30-day period within the first 3 months**, with in-person re-evaluation **no sooner than day 31, no later than day 91**.

**A gate that applies to every RAD pathway and gets missed:** the record must document **symptoms characteristic of sleep-associated hypoventilation** — daytime hypersomnolence, fatigue, morning headache, cognitive dysfunction, dyspnea. No symptoms, no RAD.

### Slide 20 — Matching device to clinical problem (the payoff slide)

| Problem | First-line | Escalate when | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated OSA** | **CPAP/APAP**, not bilevel | CPAP fails *despite* optimal fit and pressure | Patil 2019 (AASM) suggests CPAP/APAP **over** bilevel |
| **Stable ambulatory OHS with severe OSA** | **CPAP** | Persistent hypercapnia despite adherence → BPAP S/T | Mokhlesi 2019 (ATS), conditional |
| **Hospitalized, suspected OHS** | **Discharge on NIV** pending evaluation and titration | — | Mokhlesi 2019 — do **not** generalize to stable outpatients |
| **COPD, persistent hypercapnia after recovery** | **BPAP with backup rate, high-intensity, targeting real PaCO₂ reduction** | FiO₂ needs, >8 h/day, alarms+battery, or RAD targets unachievable | Köhnlein 2014; Murphy 2017; Macrea 2020 (ATS) |
| **NMD / chest wall** | **BPAP (S or S/T) early** — MIP/FVC criteria, don't wait for CO₂ | Daytime/mouthpiece support, >10 h/day, dependence | Ward 2005; Khan 2023 (CHEST) |
| **CSA** | Depends on etiology — CPAP, backup-rate bilevel, or ASV | — | Badr 2025 (AASM); **bilevel *without* backup is suggested against** |

**COPD trial contrast — keep it to three rows, the lesson is selection and timing, not device labels:**

| Trial | Population / timing | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Köhnlein 2014** | Stable severe hypercapnic COPD; NIV targeted substantial PaCO₂ reduction | 1-yr mortality **12% vs 33%**; **ARR 21%, NNT 5** |
| **RESCUE (Struik 2014)** | Hypercapnia >48 h after stopping acute ventilatory support | No improvement in readmission-or-death despite better CO₂ |
| **HOT-HMV (Murphy 2017)** | Persistent hypercapnia **2–4 weeks** after resolution of acidosis | Median time to readmission/death **1.4 → 4.3 months**; adj HR **0.49 (0.31–0.77)** |

⚠️ **Do not reproduce ONMAP's "36% relative reduction" for Köhnlein — it is arithmetically wrong.** Use ARR 21% / NNT 5.
⚠️ **HOT-HMV tested a home NIV *strategy*. It did not randomize E0466 against E0471.**

### Slide 21 — The prescription is a care plan, not a brand name

```
Indication & goal → mode and settings → interface & oxygen → safety needs → training → who reassesses, and when
```

Three distinct follow-up questions, routinely collapsed into one: **Is the patient using it? Is it providing effective support? Is the patient benefiting?** Download adherence answers only the first.

- For a dependent patient: an explicit **interruption-response plan**. Note that CMS supports a second device only for the wheelchair-mounted + bedside scenario — *"documentation that states the need for a second device as backup in case of device failure is not supported by CMS"* — even though patients requiring >18 h/day are functionally ventilator-dependent.
- For a patient started during acute illness: an explicit **reassessment plan**, including whether the diagnosis and the device requirement still hold.
- **Do not put these in a justification letter — they will not work:** portability, need for an internal battery due to unstable home power, air travel, multimodal support (cough assist/nebulizer/suction), or improvement on a hospital ventilator running settings the patient's own bilevel could deliver.

**New, and worth flagging:** effective **October 28, 2026**, E0466/E0467/E0468 join the **Face-to-Face Encounter and Written Order Prior to Delivery** list. A home NIV ordered without a documented pre-delivery visit and written order is not payable — regardless of NCD 240.9. (E0465 and the RAD codes are not on the list. This is *not* prior authorization; nothing here is on the Required Prior Authorization List, and no respiratory category is in competitive bidding Round 2028.)

### Slide 22 — Return to the three patients

Ask the room, for each: **physiologic goal → required capabilities → coverage route.**

Expected answers: **(A)** CPAP, E0601, L33718. **(B)** high-intensity BPAP S/T, E0471, NCD 240.9 — with the discharge pathway if she leaves the hospital on it. **(C)** starts as a RAD on FVC/MIP criteria under L33800; becomes an HMV when daytime mouthpiece support, battery, and alarms become the need.

**Three things to retain:**
1. **A treatment mode is not a device class.** Bi-Level S/T exists on both sides of the reimbursement line.
2. **Choose capabilities from clinical need and the consequence of interruption** — battery, alarms, daytime interface, invasive route. Everything else has converged.
3. **The categories are historical artifacts, not physiology.** A 1992 marketing sentence, a 1993 statute, and a 1998 spending alarm. Knowing that is what lets you argue with the denial letter.
